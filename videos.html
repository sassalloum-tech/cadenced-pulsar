Tiny Passengers: A Galactic Fairy Tale About the Bacteria That Fly With Us

Hostile and closed environments

NASA lists monitoring microbial communities and tracking immune responses under this hazard. Also touches: distance from Earth.

This video has not been added yet.
SI-generated with Canva

What you see

An animated story told by a galaxy fairy. Cartoon bacteria live in a gut, a few sit under falling droplets, and at the end Earth floats inside a protective bubble.

In plain words

  • Bacteria live on and inside every person. Most are harmless or even helpful, like many of the ones in our gut.
  • In space, an astronaut's immune system does not work as well as on Earth. NASA studies describe a lasting disturbance of the immune system, which suggests a higher risk of infection.
  • Bacteria can change in space too. In experiments they grew differently, formed sticky layers called biofilms, and reacted differently to antibiotics. One germ that causes food poisoning, Salmonella, became better at causing illness in mice. The results are not the same for every kind of bacteria.

Why it matters for astronauts

Astronauts on the space station have had skin, urinary and breathing infections. Their gut and skin bacteria shift during long flights, but scientists do not yet know what every shift means for health. On a long mission, a crew far from Earth must notice an infection early and choose treatment carefully, because antibiotics also harm helpful bacteria.

How ASCIS uses this

ASCIS compares temperature, breathing rate and heart rate with each astronaut's own baseline. This matters because resting body temperature runs about 1 °C higher in orbit, so a textbook fever limit would be wrong. NASA also screens crews for the resistant germ MRSA with a simple nose swab before and after flight.

Sources

  • NASA Technical Reports Server 20230014528: Spaceflight-induced changes in microbial virulence and the impact to the host immune response.
  • NASA Technical Reports Server 20210022952: microbial responses to spaceflight, including antibiotic resistance and Salmonella virulence.
  • NASA Medical Requirements Integration Documents, MEDB 2.4: MRSA nasal screen and suppression (JSC28913).
  • American Society for Microbiology, "Out of This World: Microbes in Space" (2022).
  • Stahn et al., Scientific Reports, 2017: core body temperature in long-duration spaceflight.

Hit. Fix. Float Free: The Bracelet That Casts a Web in Zero-G

Gravity fields

Weightlessness shifts the fluids and takes away anything to push against. Also touches: hostile and closed environments.

This video has not been added yet.
SI-generated with Canva

What you see

A bracelet on the astronaut's wrist fires a tether. The anchor hits a socket on the wall and fixes itself there. Now steady, the astronaut uses a handheld device on the legs. The on-screen words mention lymphatic drainage and fluid movement. This is a concept and has not been built.

In plain words

  • In weightlessness nothing holds you down. Every push you make pushes you back, so even small jobs send you drifting.
  • The web gives you an anchor, so you stay where you are while you work, scan or help someone.
  • On Earth, gravity pulls blood toward the legs. In orbit, about 2 liters of fluid shift toward the upper body, and the legs shrink. NASA tests thigh cuffs, compression garments and lower body negative pressure to move fluid back down.

The physics check

Assumptions: an astronaut with suit of 100 kg, and a station of 420,000 kg.

IdeaMathResult
Every push has an equal push back (Newton's third law)a = F ÷ m = 20 N ÷ 100 kgA free astronaut drifts 0.9 m in 3 s. Tied to the station, the station moves 4,200 times less, so the tether holds.
Firing the anchor (momentum is kept)0.2 kg × 5 m/s = 1 kg·m/s. Recoil = 1 ÷ 100The astronaut recoils only 1 cm/s.
The "hit" (impulse)F = Δp ÷ Δt = 1 ÷ 0.01 sAbout 100 N for a hundredth of a second, so the wall socket must hold more than that.
Reeling in gentlyT = m × a = 100 × 0.1. Time = 2 × √(5 ÷ 0.1)10 N of pull moves you 5 m in about 14 s, at a top speed of 0.7 m/s.
Why legs and fluid change in orbitΔP = ρ g h = 1060 × 9.81 × 1.3 mOn Earth the blood pressure at the ankle is about 100 mmHg higher than at the heart. In orbit g is nearly 0, so that gradient disappears and fluid moves up.

An honest note

The video says the device can boost the immune system. That is a reasonable idea: the lymph system is the body's immune highway, carrying fluid and immune cells through the lymph nodes, so helping lymph move could support the body's defenses. The sources we found support moving fluid. Direct proof that this device boosts immunity in astronauts is still missing, so we present it as a promising idea to test.

How ASCIS uses this

A steady body gives cleaner heart traces and ultrasound pictures, so ASCIS can tell a real warning from a false one. It also makes CPR and moving a patient safer.

Sources

  • NASA Human Health and Performance, Cardiovascular and Vision: headward fluid shift of about 2 liters, and countermeasures (lower body negative pressure, thigh cuffs, compression garments).
  • NASA Technical Reports Server 20230000939: veno-constrictive thigh cuffs and the headward fluid shift.
  • Newton's laws of motion, momentum and impulse (standard physics). Hydrostatic pressure ΔP = ρgh, with 1 mmHg = 133.3 Pa.

Dr. Axo to the Rescue: One Golden Rule for a Broken Arm in Zero-G

Distance from Earth

When Mission Control is far away, the first minutes of first aid decide the outcome. Also touches: gravity fields (bones).

This video has not been added yet.
SI-generated with Canva

What you see

An astronaut holds an injured arm. A pink axolotl doctor floats in with a first-aid bag, fits a metal splint and a bandage, and teaches rule number one: never push the bone back into place. Mission Control joins on a screen.

In plain words

  • A fracture is a break in a bone. The first job is to keep the arm still, so the break does not move and hurt the nerves and blood vessels around it.
  • Rule number one: do not push or pull the bone back into place. Without training, you can cause permanent damage.
  • Tie a stiff splint above and below the break, with padding. Then check the fingers: they should be warm and able to feel. Cold or numb fingers mean the bandage is too tight.
  • For pain, NASA's fracture plan puts acetaminophen first, because anti-inflammatory pills may slow bone healing.
  • Call Mission Control for advice.

Why it matters for astronauts

NASA's fracture form notes that no fracture has been published from spaceflight so far, but longer missions with moon and Mars walks raise the chance. In flight there is no X-ray, CT or MRI, and an ultrasound may help. NASA calls splinting a necessity and talking with ground specialists necessary for complicated breaks. NASA's plan assumes a physician-level crew member may attempt to realign a badly displaced bone. Everyone else follows rule number one.

The physics check

Assumptions: an 80 kg person, an arm of about 4 kg (5% of body mass), and 1 kg·m/s² = 1 N.

IdeaMathResult
Weightless is not masslessWeight W = m g = 4 × 9.81. Push F = 10 N: a = F ÷ m = 10 ÷ 4On Earth the arm weighs 39 N and pulls on the break. In orbit that pull is nearly zero, but a 10 N bump still whips the arm at 2.5 m/s², so it must be held still.
Every pull has an equal pull backa = F ÷ m = 50 N ÷ 80 kgA helper tugging with 50 N is pushed back at 0.63 m/s², drifting 0.31 m in 1 s. So never yank a bone, and anchor yourself first.
Why a stiff splint helpsStiffness depends on thickness cubed (I = b·h³ ÷ 12)A splint twice as thick is 2³ = 8 times stiffer, so a thick strip holds a break far better than a thin one.
Bones adapt to the load they carryStanding load = 80 × 9.81On Earth the legs carry about 785 N. In orbit it is nearly 0 N, so bones get less work. NASA requires 2.5 hours of exercise a day (1 hour aerobic and 1.5 hours resistive).

How ASCIS uses this

ASCIS cannot see a broken bone. It watches the astronaut for pain and shock (a fast heart rate or a falling blood pressure), keeps a log of what was done, tracks supplies such as pain medicine, and queues the message for Earth. The stabilization web also steadies the helper.

Sources

  • NASA Integrated Medical Model, Clinical Finding Form: Fracture, Distal Leg (2023): no published in-flight fractures, splinting as a necessity, acetaminophen first, limits of imaging in flight, ground consultation.
  • NASA MEDB 5.2, On-Orbit Strength and Conditioning (JSC28913): daily exercise requirement.
  • Newton's laws of motion; beam bending stiffness (I = b·h³ ÷ 12); standard body-segment mass fractions.

The Pocket Pharmacy: Four Glowing Vials and a Pump That Never Acts Alone

Distance from Earth

When help is far away, the crew must give medicine safely on its own. Also touches: isolation and confinement.

This video has not been added yet.
SI-generated with Canva

What you see

The pink doctor and the fairy show a wearable set of four glowing vials and a bedside pump with four channels and a touch screen. The screen shows live vital signs. An astronaut rests on a medical bed with an oxygen mask, a lung hologram floats beside the doctor, and a crewmate stays close. This is a concept and has not been built.

In plain words

  • A pump is a machine that pushes liquid medicine into a vein at exactly the right speed. Each channel holds its own medicine and its own speed, and a bolus button gives one quick extra dose.
  • The screen also shows heart rate, oxygen, blood pressure, temperature and CO₂, so the crew can see how the body answers each dose.
  • A person always stays in charge: the pump shows the dose, waits, and gives it only when a human says yes.

Why it matters for astronauts

A call to Earth can take up to 22 minutes each way. NASA's acute coronary syndrome form lists IV access, oxygen and clot-busting treatment among the care the crew could give, and it says cath-lab procedures are outside what flight can do. A careful pump lets the crew treat while Earth advises.

The physics check

Assumptions: saline-like liquid (1005 kg/m³, 1 mPa·s), a standard 18-gauge IV catheter (inner diameter about 0.84 mm, length 45 mm), a 10 mL syringe (area about 1.65 cm²).

IdeaMathResult
Why not a drip bag?Pressure from a hanging bag: ΔP = ρ g h = 1005 × 9.81 × 1 mOn Earth a bag hung 1 m up pushes with about 9.9 kPa (74 mmHg). In orbit g is nearly 0, so the push disappears and the drip stops. A pump pushes with a piston instead.
How hard must the piston push?Flow Q = 7 mL ÷ 5 s = 1.4 mL/s. Poiseuille: ΔP = 8 μ L Q ÷ (π r⁴). Force = ΔP × areaAbout 5.2 kPa (39 mmHg) through the catheter, so the piston needs only about 0.85 N, like holding up a 90 g object, plus a little friction.
A slow channel2.5 mL/h from a 50 mL syringe (area about 5.5 cm²). Time = 50 ÷ 2.5. Piston speed = Q ÷ areaThe syringe lasts 20 hours, and the piston creeps only about 4.5 mm per hour.
Why a bubble detector?Buoyant force = ρ g VWith g near 0 there is almost no buoyancy, so air bubbles do not float away from the line. The pump must detect air and stop.

An honest note

The words on the pump's screen in this video were garbled by the picture maker. For example, it shows "Drug Delvert Systum," misspelled drug names, and the same word on every channel. Please do not read the drug names or doses on that screen: the video is a concept. The doses in ASCIS come from the physician-reviewed list.

How ASCIS uses this

In ASCIS the treatment pump is simulated. It shows the doses worked out for the astronaut's weight, counts down ten seconds, and has STOP and Confirm buttons side by side. Every medicine given is subtracted from the ship inventory, and low supplies are queued as an alert for Earth.

Sources

  • NASA Integrated Medical Model, Clinical Finding Form: Acute Coronary Syndrome (2023): treatment scope and limits in flight.
  • Hydrostatic pressure (ΔP = ρgh), Poiseuille's law for flow in a tube, and buoyancy (standard physics). 1 mmHg = 133.3 Pa.
  • TNKase (tenecteplase) prescribing information: 50 mg vial at 5 mg/mL, so a 35 mg dose is 7 mL.
